Rapid Infrastructure and Service AssessmentMethodology noteOctober 2026

Knowing what’s on the ground, in the first days

RISA turns drone imagery and direct field observation into one operational map of a displacement site, within days of the site being set up. It answers three questions: can we reach people, which services still work, and where do we act first.

Read the three-page product · How the method works

1Why RISA

The first days decide the response. They are also when we know least.

The first 72 hours. Access routes, site layout and damage are often unknown, out of date or anecdotal, just when the most important decisions are made.

Integration, not technology. Drones, satellites and mobile data collection already exist. They are deployed separately, so the picture arrives late or in pieces.

Capacity that stays. Surge teams arrive, assess and leave. RISA is built to be handed over, so national teams can run the next assessment themselves.

2The method

Three inputs, one common operational picture.

RISA records what physically exists on the ground. It complements, rather than replaces, MIRA and rapid needs assessments. Three inputs are collected in parallel and combined on one map.

Aerial imagery (HI/Atlas Logistique). A drone orthomosaic of the site, flown at 120 m for about 4 cm per pixel, or recent satellite imagery where flying isn’t possible.

Field observation (Kobo). A standard KoboCollect survey, recorded by direct observation rather than interviews, that works offline and syncs when there’s signal.

GIS integration (MapAction). Both are brought together on one map with common symbology: a georeferenced inventory of every water point, latrine and service.

Table 1. The six steps of the standard operating procedure
StepWhat happens
1. Mission preparationScope, permits, flight authorisation and the GEO zone unlock, done before travelling.

The initial assessment is produced 3 to 5 days after a site is set up, with a follow-up about a week later and updates as the site evolves.1

4The product

Three pages, read in under a minute.

The product is a mission-ready document, not a research report. It works printed and offline, so a coordinator can brief from it in an OSOCC or in the field. An interactive version is being piloted alongside it for when there is a connection.

Page 1 is the map: site layout, access routes by status, services, hazards and logistics points. Page 2 sets out the key findings on access, essential services and priority gaps. Page 3, which is optional, is a close-up of the most urgent area with before and after imagery and recommended actions.

6Training and handover

Built to be run by national teams.

A Training of Trainers course prepares a national cohort, who then train enumerators, site managers and drone teams. Every course starts with a SIMEX: a full simulation on realistic scenario data. Drone capacity is built with the Flying Labs network of local operators.2

Handover happens in three stages. Consortium-led: national counterparts shadow every step of a deployment. Co-led: national teams lead, with remote support from the consortium. Nationally led: the consortium is the backstop for large or complex events.

7Where RISA has been used

Mozambique, January 2026 (SADC SIMEX). The three inputs were collected in parallel and combined into the three-page product. The static PDF worked, but lost much of the richness of the combined data, which led to the interactive pilot.

Venezuela, earthquake response. The drone orthomosaic showed handwashing stations, latrines, child-friendly spaces and health services that satellite imagery could not reliably show.3
